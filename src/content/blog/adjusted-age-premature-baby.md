If your baby arrived early, you have probably already had the disorienting experience of opening a milestone checklist and finding your child "behind" on almost everything. It is one of the cruellest small experiences of preemie parenthood — and it is usually a measurement error, not a developmental one.

The fix has a name: **adjusted age**, also called corrected age. Here is what it is, how to work it out, and why it changes the way you should read every chart and checklist you meet in the first two years.

## What is adjusted age?

**Adjusted age is your baby's age counted from their original due date rather than their birthday.** It answers a different question from the one on the birth certificate: not "how long has this baby been alive?" but "how far along is this baby's development?"

A baby born at 32 weeks was due eight weeks later. At four months old on the calendar, that baby has only had about two months of post-due-date development. Comparing them to a typical four-month-old is comparing two children at genuinely different stages, and the checklist that results tells you nothing useful.

## How to calculate adjusted age

The arithmetic is simple:

1. Start with **chronological age** — how long since the birthday, in weeks or months.
2. Work out **how early the baby was**: 40 weeks minus the gestational age at birth.
3. Subtract the second number from the first.

**Adjusted age = chronological age − weeks born early**

A worked example. A baby born at 30 weeks was 10 weeks early. At six months old (about 26 weeks), the adjusted age is 26 − 10 = 16 weeks, or roughly **four months adjusted**. When you read a milestone list for that baby, four months is the row to look at — not six.

| Born at | Weeks early | At 6 months old, adjusted age is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 37 weeks | 3 | ~5 months |
| 34 weeks | 6 | ~4.5 months |
| 32 weeks | 8 | ~4 months |
| 28 weeks | 12 | ~3 months |

## Where adjusted age matters most

**Milestones.** Rolling, sitting, babbling, walking — these track developmental time, not calendar time. A preemie meeting milestones on their adjusted schedule is, in the ordinary case, doing exactly what you would hope.

**Growth charts.** Plotting a preemie's weight against their chronological age will drag them down the percentiles for reasons that have nothing to do with how they are feeding. Corrected plotting is what makes the curve readable. (We wrote a whole separate guide to reading those curves: [what your baby's growth percentile actually means](/blog/baby-growth-percentiles).)

**Sleep and feeding expectations.** Sleep consolidation and feeding volumes follow developmental maturity too. "Should be sleeping through by now" is a sentence that deserves correcting before you take it to heart.

**Vaccinations are the exception.** Immunisations are almost always scheduled by chronological age, not adjusted — a preemie generally gets their shots on the ordinary calendar schedule. This is one of the few places where the birthday, not the due date, is the right number. Your pediatrician will confirm the schedule for your child.

## How long do you keep adjusting?

The usual guidance is to correct until around **two years old**, by which point most children born preterm have caught up enough that the difference stops being meaningful. Some clinicians keep correcting a little longer for babies born very early, or for specific measurements like head circumference.

The honest answer is that the cut-off is a convention rather than a hard line, and your pediatrician's view of your particular child beats any general rule you read online — including this one.

## The emotional part nobody puts in the guide

Adjusted age is not a consolation prize or a way of moving the goalposts. It is the more accurate measurement. Using it is not being generous to your baby; it is being correct about them.
